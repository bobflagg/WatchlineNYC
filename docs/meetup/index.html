(~45s) NYC ownership is fragmented by design. Each building sits in its own single-purpose LLC, usually named after the address — so on paper there's no "landlord," just a scatter of shells. Take Steven Croman: a landlord the AG investigated, who pleaded guilty and served jail time — about as public and documented as a bad actor gets. In Who Owns What's OWN Croman portfolio, those 118 buildings are held under 107 different corporate names — nothing in that list says "Croman." Tools like Who Owns What already re-assemble a lot of this — that's what I build on. But if it takes work to see it for a FAMOUS operator, the quiet ones are effectively invisible — to tenants, reporters, and the agencies meant to hold them accountable.

(~55s) The baseline everyone here knows — JustFix's Who Owns What, the open-source tool this builds on. The key thing to see is that it's a GRAPH problem. NODES first: every building files an HPD registration, and each registration lists contacts — head officer, owner, agent. WoW takes the owner/officer contact and makes a node keyed on that person's name plus their standardized business address — so a node is a (name, business-address) pulled straight from the registrations and contacts tables. EDGES next: draw one between two nodes on either of two rules — CONNECTED_BY_ADDRESS when they share a business office, or CONNECTED_BY_NAME when they share a name backed by a nearby address. Then a "portfolio" is just a CONNECTED COMPONENT of that graph — everything transitively linked, like the four ringed here; the two on the right are a different component, a different portfolio. Notice what that component actually captures: an OPERATIONAL NEXUS — who registers and manages together — not title. A genuinely strong first pass, and what makes the invisible visible. But hold onto the mechanism, because it's exactly where it breaks: lean on a shared address and you fuse unrelated owners who merely use the same managing agent — a FALSE MERGE; require a shared address or name and a real owner who leaves neither drops off the graph entirely — a FALSE SPLIT. Those two are the rest of the talk.
The problem this talk is about

Two ways to be wrong

Both come from the same rule — connect on shared attributes. Lean on it and you fuse strangers; require it and you lose real owners. The rest of the talk: fix each without breaking the other.
(~45s) The same mechanism from the last slide breaks in exactly two directions. FALSE MERGE, on the left: three buildings with three different real owners all list the same business address — because they all use the same managing agent. WoW's CONNECTED_BY_ADDRESS rule links them, the connected component swallows all three, and now one "portfolio" is really three owners — you over-count, and you can smear one owner's violations onto another. FALSE SPLIT, on the right: one owner holds three buildings in three shell LLCs, each with a different registered name and a different office — nothing to match on, so there are NO edges. WoW sees three separate components — three portfolios — and the real owner is fragmented, effectively invisible. Same rule, opposite failures: lean on shared attributes and you merge strangers; require them and you split a real owner. The whole rest of the talk is how we fix each of these — WITHOUT trading one failure for the other.

The engine — fixing false splits

Splink — probabilistic record linkage

Decide whether two records are the same owner from many partial clues — not one exact match

CROMAN · 9 EAST 38 STREET STE 600 · 10016
last name exact & rare ✓✓ · street fuzzy ~ · zip exact ✓  →  match probability ≈ 0.99  →  same owner (the exact-match rule would split these)
①Compares field-by-field, in degrees — exact / fuzzy / mismatch on name, house number, street, zip — so typos and variants still match
②Weighs the evidence (Fellegi–Sunter model) — agreement on a rare value counts more than a common one → a calibrated match probability, with weights learned unsupervised (no labels)
③Scales, with a precision dial — “blocking” compares only plausible pairs, so it runs over millions of records; we set the threshold high — link only on strong evidence
The open-source engine behind our CONNECTED_BY_SPLINK edges — it links the fragments exact-match rules miss, reuniting an owner WoW splits.
Splink — open-source probabilistic record linkage, UK Ministry of Justice · moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink
(~55s) So how do we fix that false split? The identity engine is Splink — an open-source probabilistic record-linkage library from the UK Ministry of Justice. Not our invention; a well-established tool. The idea: instead of demanding an exact name-and-address match like the baseline, it compares two records field by field in DEGREES — exact, fuzzy, or mismatch — and weighs each agreement by how much evidence it actually carries. Agreeing on a rare surname is strong; agreeing on a common one is weak. It sums those into a calibrated match PROBABILITY, and it learns those weights unsupervised — no labeled training data. Blocking keeps it fast over millions of records. Look at Croman's two office registrations: "9 E 38 St" versus "9 East 38 Street Ste 600." The exact-match rule sees two different addresses and splits them; Splink sees the same rare name, a fuzzy street match, the same zip, and says 0.99 — same owner. And it's a DIAL: we set the threshold high, precision-first, so we only merge on strong evidence — which matters, because the other failure mode is merging strangers.

(~45s) Splink fixed Croman because his records shared SOMETHING — a rare name, a fuzzy address. But some owners leave nothing to match on. Ten identical townhouses on one block of Haight Street in Flushing: Who Owns What reads the registered nine as SIX different owners — six head officers, nine LLCs, no shared name or address — so name, address, AND Splink all split them. The only tie is a transaction: in 2020 a bankruptcy trustee sold the whole row — all ten — on one deed, $18.6 million, to a single buyer, who re-deeded them one at a time into shells. So we add a fourth edge type, CONNECTED_BY_DEED, straight from ACRIS, and it reunites nine of the ten — a tie that is pure deed, no name, no address. Who Owns What can't reach this even in principle: it reads no deeds. Then the honesty beat — the tenth, 41-23, is on the same deed but has no HPD registration, so it falls through BOTH systems: the ghost dot. Precision-first, with a real recall limit shown on purpose.

(~55s) Now the other failure — the false merge — and it's the answer to the obvious challenge, "doesn't your system just merge everything into big portfolios?" No — here it does the opposite. This is Who Owns What's portfolio #183: 27 buildings it labels as one owner, Nathan Obstfeld. Recolor them by who actually owns them and it's seven different owners across three boroughs. What glues them in WoW is a single shared office in Lakewood, New Jersey — and I checked, it's not a registered agent, just an address about ten small landlords self-file from. So how do we keep the useful signal without the false empire? We stop collapsing everything into one "portfolio," because a portfolio is really THREE questions. Who MANAGES — self-disclosed, near-sourced. What operational NEXUS it runs through — that's where the shared Lakewood office belongs, WoW's real strength, an inference we keep. And who OWNS it — a separate, precision-first layer that never merges on a shared address, so Miller resolves to seven. Same office and same owner live in DIFFERENT layers. And to be honest, the identity layer isn't magic — it left one owner, Obtfeld, split off on a one-letter typo. Precision-first, and it diverges from WoW both ways.

Appendix · the live agent

Conversational — by design, not free-form

The agent orchestrates queries; it doesn't reason about people

Guarantees enforced in code — not asked of the model:

  • Asks the graph, never answers from itself — plain English → parameterized, read-only queries; it reports only what a query returned, every inferred element carrying its caveat.
  • "Who owns this?" returns both — recorded owner and apparent controller, labeled — and flags when they disagree.
  • Read-only, access-gated — writes/admin refused; deep investigation only for vetted users (fail-closed).
You can't manufacture false certainty about a person if you only ever report what a sourced, labeled query returned.
Enforced in the tool-visibility layer, not a prompt — the records themselves (violation text, ACRIS JSON) are a prompt-injection surface.
(~35s) If we go to the live tool — a note on what the agent is first. It's an ORCHESTRATOR, not a reasoner. It turns your question into a parameterized, read-only query — it doesn't free-associate an answer, and it reports only what the query returned, caveats attached. "Who owns this?" always returns both the recorded owner and the apparent controller, labeled. And it's read-only with gated access — enforced in the code that decides which tools the model can even see, not in a prompt, because these records are full of free text an adversary could hide instructions in. Orchestrator, not reasoner.

Backup · likely pushback

Two fair questions

"We live in SQL — what does the graph add?"
  • Concede: most of the pipeline is SQL — WoW is Postgres, the linkage is DuckDB.
  • The graph earns its place for the transitive, multi-relationship parts: connected-component owner identity · N-hop "who connects to whom" across owner/manager/deed layers · graph analytics.
  • "Count violations per owner" → SQL. "Everyone within 3 hops across four link types, ranked by centrality" → graph.
"We don't trust AI."
  • Good — neither do I, for claims about people.
  • The real inference is transparent statistical linkage (measured precision), not a neural net.
  • The language model is a read-only front door — it makes no claims; the public record does. Ignore it and read the sourced records.
  • Its main job is to not falsely connect people.
Both answers share one core: the substance is the public record + a transparent, measured linkage step. The graph and the LLM are tooling around it — each earning its place, everything labeled sourced vs. inferred.
SQL question — concede hard: most of this IS SQL. WoW is Postgres, Splink is DuckDB, every conditions aggregate is a GROUP BY. The graph earns its place in three spots that are awkward or slow in SQL, not impossible: (1) transitive closure — the owner-identity layer IS connected components over the identity edges, one call in a graph, a recursive-CTE headache in SQL; (2) heterogeneous N-hop traversal — "start at this building, walk out across shared-owner/manager/address/deed links, who do I reach" is one graph pattern, a per-hop per-edge-type union in SQL; (3) graph data science — centrality, community, link-prediction run natively. If someone says "you could do it with recursive CTEs or Apache AGE" — concede: you can; it's a convenience/performance + agent/GDS substrate call, not a capability SQL lacks. AI question — AGREE with them; skepticism is correct. Separate the two AIs: the thing that decides who's the same owner is an auditable STATISTICAL model whose precision we measure — not a neural net; the language model is only the front door and never invents an answer, it reads the record and labels it. "If you don't trust the AI, ignore it — read the sourced records it points you to; the findings don't depend on it." And the trust-builder: the tool's main job is to NOT falsely connect people (the Miller case). Name the honest limit yourself: the accuracy eval isn't run yet.
