Case studies

The layers in practice

Three worked examples of the owner-identity layer doing its job — merging one owner the record splits, and (twice) refusing to merge parties the record only appears to connect. The answer, in data, to "doesn't it just merge everything?"
Leads, not verdicts. Ownership and control links below are algorithmic inferences from public records — investigative leads to verify, not determinations of legal ownership. Recorded owners, court records, and managing agents are directly sourced. The two "un-merge" cases below separate parties the record only appears to connect — the favorable, cautious direction. All names are drawn from public NYC filings.

Watchline separates three questions the record often conflates — who owns a building, what operational network it runs through, and who manages it. These cases show each boundary holding. (See also the flagship Steven Croman case file.)

1 · One owner, split by a typo merge

Who Owns What splits landlord Ramon Escobar's Bronx portfolio into two portfolios that carry the identical name and business address — because a single registration-address typo (2432 GRAND COURSE 504 vs …CONCOURSE…) fractured its connection graph. Watchline keeps all 26 buildings together as one owner.

Three adjacent Creston Avenue buildings, all deed-owned by the same entity, land in different WoW portfolios purely on the typo. The ACRIS held-deed signal sourced plus name-anchored resolution reunites them. Layer: owner-identity de-fragmentation. 2 · One office, seven owners un-merge · address

Who Owns What groups 27 buildings as a single owner (its portfolio #183, labeled "Nathan Obstfeld") — but they are really ~10 different owners who merely share one registration office at 235 River Ave, Lakewood NJ. Watchline separates them into the seven distinct owners they actually are.

The office is a shared suite, not a registered agent and not one operator — so "same office" is not "same owner." Layer: operational nexus ≠ ownership. Explore the interactive map (toggle Watchline ↔ Who Owns What).

This is the direct answer to "doesn't it just merge everything?" — here it does the opposite, correctly.

3 · One manager, five separate owners un-merge · manager

Five buildings all filed from one small office (240 Riverside Blvd, unit CU2) and all managed by Verus Real Estate. The single constant is the managing agent — every recorded owner and every apparent controller differs. So the shared thread is management, not ownership.

Watchline puts none of these in one owner group; it splits them into three portfolios grouped by their apparent owners, and keeps the shared operator only in the management layer. If it had merged them, it would almost certainly have been wrong — the deeds name five unrelated parties. Layer: management ≠ ownership.

One message across all three: the owner-identity layer is precision-first. It keeps who owns cleanly separate from who operates through this office and who manages the building — merging a true owner the record fragments, and declining to merge parties the record only appears to connect.
