Case studies

The layers in practice

Four worked examples of the owner-identity layer doing its job — merging owners the record splits (a typo; a shared deed), and refusing to merge parties it only appears to connect (a shared office; a shared manager). The answer, in data, to "doesn't it just merge everything?"
Leads, not verdicts. Ownership and control links below are algorithmic inferences from public records — investigative leads to verify, not determinations of legal ownership. Recorded owners, deeds, court records, and managing agents are directly sourced; who controls or owns across them is inferred. Where a case merges parties (Escobar, Haight), that a shared origin means one present owner is a lead an investigator confirms — never a verdict. All names are drawn from public NYC filings.

Watchline separates three questions the record often conflates — who owns a building, what operational network it runs through, and who manages it. These cases show each boundary holding. (See also the flagship Steven Croman case file.)

1 · One owner, split by a typo merge

Who Owns What splits landlord Ramon Escobar's Bronx portfolio into two portfolios that carry the identical name and business address — because a single registration-address typo (2432 GRAND COURSE 504 vs …CONCOURSE…) fractured its connection graph. Watchline keeps all 26 buildings together as one owner.

Who Owns What groups 27 buildings as a single owner (its portfolio #183, labeled "Nathan Obstfeld") — but they are really ~10 different owners who merely share one registration office at 235 River Ave, Lakewood NJ. Watchline separates them into the seven distinct owners they actually are.

This is the direct answer to "doesn't it just merge everything?" — here it does the opposite, correctly.

3 · One manager, five separate owners un-merge · manager

Five buildings whose one constant is the managing agent — Anya Levitov / Verus Real Estate, named on every registration. The recorded owners and apparent controllers all differ, and the owners even file from different offices (33 West 60th St; 240 Riverside Blvd CU2) — so the shared thread is management, not ownership.

4 · One block, six owners, one deed merge · deed

Nine identical 4-unit townhouses on one Flushing block (41-09 → 41-27 Haight Street, Queens). Who Owns What reads them as six separate owners — six registration head officers (Chan, Xu, Ho, Lin, Li, Chen) and nine different DOF owner-of-record LLCs. Nothing it keys on is shared, so it splits the block six ways. Watchline reunites all nine into one owner group — on evidence Who Owns What never reads.

The tie is a transaction. In 2020 a Chapter 11 bankruptcy trustee sold the entire row — all nine parcels on one deed sourced — for $18.6M to a single buyer, 41 Haight Street Townhouse Owner, LLC. That buyer then re-deeded seven of the parcels, one at a time, into single-purpose shells (one was never moved). Watchline's CONNECTED_BY_DEED veil-pierce inferred recovers the common origin the re-titling obscured. Layer: owner identity, read from the deed record.

Here Who Owns What isn't wrong — it's blind: the only tie is a transaction, and it reads no deeds. A shared 2020 origin and a consistent re-deeding-from-the-parent pattern are strong common-control signals — a lead to verify, not proof of one present beneficial owner.
One message across all four: the owner-identity layer is precision-first. It keeps who owns cleanly separate from who operates through this office and who manages the building — merging a true owner the record fragments (even when the only trace is a deed), and declining to merge parties the record only appears to connect.
